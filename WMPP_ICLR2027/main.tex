Because these approaches use different algorithmic structures, rely on different assumptions, and induce different behaviors, their relative performance varies across environments, goals, and task horizons; no single method consistently dominates \citep{park2025ogbench}.

Goal-conditioned reinforcement learning (GCRL) seeks policies that reach user-specified goals, many of which require a sequence of qualitatively different phases: to stack two cubes at a target location, a robot arm must bring its free gripper to the first cube, grasp and place it, then repeat for the second cube.
GCRL methods learn such policies through behavior cloning, temporal-difference learning, contrastive learning, quasimetric learning, or hierarchical control \citep{eysenbach2022contrastive,wang2023quasimetric,park2023hiql}, and because their assumptions differ, no single method dominates across environments, goals, and task horizons \citep{park2025ogbench}.
Nor does one method dominate across the phases of a single task: on OGBench's two-cube tasks (\texttt{cube-double-play}), GCIQL, the strongest of six standard GCRL methods, succeeds in 36\% of episodes and HIQL in only 5\% (\cref{tab:main}), yet our method, selecting among the six policies within each episode, succeeds in 69\%.

Independently trained policies can thus act as pieces of a larger solution, and we study how to string them together at test time without retraining any of them.

Most prior work aims to train a single stronger policy, and standard practice in offline GCRL is to train several candidates, keep the one with the highest average success, and discard the rest.
In the two-cube example, this keeps GCIQL and discards HIQL, the policy that moves the gripper between cubes in \cref{fig:episode}.
Keeping all of them instead raises a basic question: \emph{given a set of frozen goal-conditioned policies, which policy should act from the current state for the current goal?}

This selection problem is challenging for three reasons.
First, the policies cannot be ranked by their own value functions.
The most direct approach would pair each policy $\pi_i$ with its value function $V_i$ and execute $\pi_{i^\star}$ with $i^\star = \arg\max_i V_i(s, g)$, a policy-level form of generalized policy improvement \citep{barreto2017successor}.
This rule presumes that every $V_i$ measures the same quantity on the same scale, which does not hold across GCRL algorithms: $V_i$ may be a temporal-difference value, a quasimetric distance, or a contrastive score, and GCBC learns none.
Second, a policy should be judged by where its closed-loop behavior leads, yet at test time only the selected one is executed, so the consequences of the alternatives are never observed.
Third, frequent switching between policies can disrupt temporally coherent behavior.

Our key idea is to compare policies through the consequences they are predicted to produce. From the current state, our method, World-Model Policy Arbiter (\wmpa), rolls out each frozen policy in a learned world model while conditioning all policies on the same final goal. It scores the resulting imagined futures with a shared goal-conditioned value function, executes the highest-scoring policy for a fixed commitment interval, and repeats this procedure. Unlike action-level model-predictive control, \wmpa{} does not optimize new action sequences; instead, it evaluates the behaviors already represented in the policy bank, which requires executing each policy just once at each imagined step, rather than a search over possible trajectories. All base policies remain frozen, and the selector requires no hand-designed progress signal, no rollouts in the real environment, and no privileged task knowledge.

\section{Goal-Conditioned Reinforcement Learning Preliminaries}
\label{sec:prelim}
Following \citet{park2025ogbench}, we consider goal-conditioned reinforcement learning over a controlled Markov process
$\mathcal{M}=(\calS,\calA,\calG,\mu,p)$.
Here, $\calS$ is the state space, $\calA$ is the action space, $\calG$ is the goal space, $\mu\in\Delta(\calS)$ is the initial-state distribution, and $p(s'\mid s,a)\colon\calS\times\calA\to\Delta(\calS)$ denotes the transition dynamics, where $\Delta(\cdot)$ denotes the space of probability distributions over its argument. A goal-conditioned policy $\pi(a\mid s,g)\colon\calS\times\calG\to\Delta(\calA)$ maps the current state and goal to a distribution over actions.

Let $\nu\in\Delta(\calS\times\calG)$ denote the evaluation distribution over initial-state and goal pairs. Given $(s_0,g)\sim\nu$, a policy induces a trajectory $\tau=(s_0,a_0,s_1,\ldots,a_{T-1},s_T)$ according to the transition dynamics. Its performance is measured by expected goal-reaching success:
\[
    J(\pi)
    =
    \mathbb{E}_{(s_0,g)\sim\nu,\,
    \tau\sim p^\pi(\tau\mid s_0,g)}
    \left[\operatorname{Succ}(\tau,g)\right],
\]
where $\operatorname{Succ}(\tau,g)\in\{0,1\}$ indicates whether $\tau$
%%NR.09.23: Succ, as defined above, doesn't need to know about T, since it's implicit in the length of \tau. So you could drop everything after "reaches $g$". JQ: dropped
reaches $g$. This success criterion is used only for evaluation and is not available during training. Instead, goal-reaching supervision is constructed through hindsight goal relabeling, using states from logged trajectories as goals.

We assume access to a logged trajectory dataset $\calD=\{\tau^{(n)}\}_{n=1}^N$, where each trajectory $\tau^{(n)}=(s_0^{(n)},a_0^{(n)},s_1^{(n)},\ldots,s_{T_n}^{(n)})$ is collected as part of an offline dataset. We use $\calD$ to train the auxiliary world model and value function. In our experiments, $\calD$ is the OGBench training split, and all policies are trained offline using the same split. The general formulation, however, does not require the policies to be trained offline or from a shared dataset.

Rather than learning a single goal-conditioned policy, we are given a portfolio of $M$ frozen policies,
\[
    \PiBank=\{\pi_i(a\mid s,g)\}_{i=1}^M,
\]
whose members may differ in the GCRL algorithm or random seed used for training. All members share a common state, action, and goal interface, and the arbiter requires only black-box access to their action distributions. Internal value estimates and representations are neither assumed to be accessible nor required to be comparable across policies.

At arbitration time $t$, the arbiter selects a policy index $z_t\in\{1,\ldots,M\}$. The selected policy then controls the agent for a commitment interval of length $c$:
\[
    a_{t+j}\sim\pi_{z_t}(\cdot\mid s_{t+j},g),
    \qquad j=0,\ldots,c-1.
\]
The resulting controller is therefore a switching policy over the frozen portfolio members. The policy-portfolio objective is to maximize the expected goal-reaching success of this induced policy without modifying any member of $\PiBank$. The central problem is thus to determine, from the current state and goal, which portfolio member is expected to produce the most favorable future trajectory.

\subsection{Learned state-space world model}
\label{sec:dynamics}
For each environment, we train an ensemble of $E$ independently parameterized MLP dynamics models~\citep{kurutach2018model} directly in the benchmark state space. Throughout this section, all states are raw benchmark states; the fixed normalizer $\nu(s)=(s-\mu_s)/\sigma_s$, whose statistics are computed from the logged trajectories, is applied only at the network input. Each member predicts a normalized one-step delta and returns the next state,
\begin{align}
\hat{\delta}^{(e)}_t &= f_{\theta_e}\big(\nu(s_t),a_t\big),\\
\hat{s}^{(e)}_{t+1} &= \hat f_{\theta_e}(s_t,a_t) := s_t + \mu_{\Delta}+\sigma_{\Delta}\hat{\delta}^{(e)}_t,
\end{align}
where $\mu_{\Delta}$ and $\sigma_{\Delta}$ are fixed statistics computed from valid logged transitions. Multi-step predictions are obtained by applying the same $\hat f_{\theta_e}$ to its own outputs, with re-normalization at every input. The outputs are trained to match non-normalized environment states and are consumed by the frozen policies exactly like real observations. The members are trained on $H$-step windows by unrolling their own predictions and penalizing the normalized error at every step,
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{dyn}} = \frac{1}{E}\sum_{e=1}^{E}
\E_{\tau\sim\calD}
\left[\sum_{h=1}^{H} w_h\left\|\nu\big(\hat{s}^{(e)}_{t+h}\big)-\nu\big(s_{t+h}\big)\right\|_2^2\right],
\end{equation}
with a curriculum that increases the effective horizon from $1$ to $H$ over the first $10^5$ updates ($w_h$ uniform over the active steps). Predicting in state space isolates arbitration from representation learning and guarantees that imagined states are valid inputs to every frozen policy. Notably, the model is decoupled from the policies: learning it can be done independently of making changes to the policy bank.

\subsection{Goal-conditioned value function}
\label{sec:value}

The arbiter scores every candidate's imagined states with a single goal-conditioned value function $V(s,g)$, so policies trained with different objectives are compared on a common scale. Only the ordering matters: futures reached by different candidates from the same decision state should be ranked in the same order as their realized outcomes. Nothing in the arbiter depends on how $V$ is parameterized, and we use two parameterizations whose inductive biases match different kinds of task progress; we choose between them per task family (\cref{sec:setup}, \cref{app:hyperparams}). We also swap the two value heads to show the performance difference and discuss value-head choice in \cref{app:ablations}.

\paragraph{Metric value (maze, cube, scene).} Where progress is geometric, like moving through a maze, displacing objects toward target poses, we use a latent distance with separate state and goal encoders for each ensemble member,
\begin{equation}
V_{\phi_e}(s,g)
= -\left\|\varphi^{(e)}_{S}(s)-\varphi^{(e)}_{G}(\psi^{(e)}(g))\right\|_2,
\label{eq:metric-value}
\end{equation}
where $\psi^{(e)}$ is a low-dimensional goal representation, following \citet{kang2026lavl}. The distance orders imagined state--goal pairs even where closed-loop rollouts leave the support of the logged data, and the distinct encoders keep reachability directional ($V_{\phi_e}(s,g)\neq V_{\phi_e}(g,s)$ in general). Each value member is trained jointly with its corresponding dynamics member by an expectile temporal-difference objective on relabeled goals with a sparse reward ($0$ at attainment and $-1$ otherwise),
%%NR.09.23: Since you don't reference all of these equations individually elsewhere (as evidenced by the lack of individual \labels), you probably should just remove the stack and have them all on one line, using \quad etc. as needed. JQ: DONE
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{value}}
= \frac{1}{E}\sum_{e=1}^{E}
\E\!\left[
\left|\kappa-\mathbf{1}\{\bar\delta_e<0\}\right|
\big(y_e-V_{\phi_e}(s,g)\big)^2
\right],
\quad
y_e = r(s,g)+\gamma\,m(s,g)\,
\bar V_{\bar\phi_e}(s',g),
\label{eq:expectile}
\end{equation}
where $\bar V$ is a target network, $m(s,g)=0$ at attainment, $\bar\delta_e=y_e-\bar V_{\bar\phi_e}(s,g)$, and the expectile satisfies $\kappa>0.5$ (settings in \cref{app:wm}).

\paragraph{Direct value (puzzle).} On the puzzle datasets progress is combinatorial: a state is a pattern of buttons, a press toggles a button together with its neighbours, and the number of presses to the goal pattern is not monotone in embedding distance. Thus, we use a value with no geometric structure,
\begin{equation}
V_{\theta}(s,g) = \mathrm{MLP}_{\theta}\big([\,s;\,g\,]\big),
\label{eq:direct-value}
\end{equation}
trained by implicit Q-learning \citep{kostrikov2022iql} on the same dataset. Two action-value heads $Q_{\omega,i}(s,a,g)$ are regressed toward the one-step target $r(s,g)+\gamma\,m(s,g)\,V_{\theta}(s',g)$, and the state value is the $\kappa$-expectile of the target value function $\bar Q=\min_i \bar Q_{\omega,i}$ under the dataset's actions,
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{V}
= \E\Big[\left|\kappa-\mathbf{1}\{\bar Q(s,a,g)-V_{\theta}(s,g)<0\}\right|\big(\bar Q(s,a,g)-V_{\theta}(s,g)\big)^2\Big],
\label{eq:direct-expectile}
\end{equation}
so that $V_{\theta}$ approximates the value of the best dataset-supported action at each relabeled state--goal pair. It is the state-value network of the bank's IQL member, trained with the reference implementation's settings and reused unchanged as the scorer. Being one function applied to every candidate's imagined states, it keeps scores comparable across the bank exactly as the metric value does.

\subsection{Model-Based Policy Rollout and ranking}
\label{sec:counterfactual}
At each arbitration point, every frozen policy is rolled out in closed loop for $k$ model steps, acting deterministically on its own imagined states. All policies in the bank are Gaussian actors whose deterministic mode is the behavior deployed and evaluated by the benchmark, so the imagined branch reproduces the behavior that would be executed; we do not evaluate stochastic rollouts obtained by sampling actions from the policy distributions. For policy $i$ and ensemble member $e$,
\begin{align}
\hat{s}^{i,e}_{0} &= s_t,\\
\hat{a}^{i,e}_{h} &= \pi_i(\hat{s}^{i,e}_{h},g),\\
\hat{s}^{i,e}_{h+1} &= \hat{f}_{\theta_e}(\hat{s}^{i,e}_{h},\hat{a}^{i,e}_{h}),
\qquad h=0,\ldots,k-1,
\end{align}
where $\hat f_{\theta_e}$ is transition model ensemble member of \cref{sec:dynamics}.
We score each candidate by the highest value imagined state along the rollout,

is a descendant of this lineage with a learned model, a truncated horizon, and goal conditioning: the model supplies the rollouts that the classical methods obtain from a simulator, and the shared value function avoids the need for the comparable per-policy action-value estimates required by GPI.
Model-based hierarchical RL is a distinct lineage. Director selects latent goals for a worker trained in imagination \citep{hafner2022director}; SkiMo plans with a skill-dynamics model learned jointly with a skill VAE \citep{shi2022skimo}; SPlaTES shapes skills to be predictable by an abstract world model and plans over them with iCEM \citep{gurtler2025splates}; Puppeteer trains a separate high-level world model for each task on top of a frozen tracking controller \citep{hansen2025puppeteer}; and a learned gate over a frozen planner decides how much search to perform \citep{muppidi2026finding}. All of these methods learn the high level, most also co-adapt the low level, and their models generate or search over the executed behavior. \wmpa{} learns neither level: the candidates are complete goal-conditioned policies trained independently with different algorithms, the dynamics model is trained once on the dataset without reference to any policy, and the model only arbitrates among them.


\section{Limitations and Future Work}
% \label{sec:limitations}
% % only use state-based datasets for now; compute overhead during test-time; failure mode ceiling cases, no complementary failures. future work could be using wm to distill abilities and as dagger to create more reliable offline data to mitigate the need of using wm during inference time so to avoid the compute overhead
% The study uses state observations; pixels would require a policy-compatible latent world model or a shared visual encoder. The auxiliary models are trained per environment, so the contribution is policy reuse, not cross-environment transfer, and the metric architecture is an inductive bias, not a guarantee, on imagined states. The per-family interval and value parameterization were fixed on held-out episodes of the same five evaluation goals; OGBench provides no further goals, so their transfer to unseen goals is untested. Random switching with a per-dataset post-hoc interval remains competitive on a few datasets where stalling policies benefit from any restart, and with a scorer that ranks candidates at chance a single dominant policy makes arbitration a net loss. All bank members are trained on the same dataset with the benchmark's reference implementations; banks that mix data or interfaces are outside the present evidence. Finally, simulation results do not establish robustness on real robots, and the bank-statistic associations rest on \NumEnvs{} datasets.

\label{sec:limitations}
This study uses state observations and per-environment transition models. Pixel observations would require either a latent world model with a shared visual encoder across all policies or a world model that produces realistic images. We leave visual environments to future work. Additionally, \wmpa{} selects among existing behaviors and cannot create new ones: when every member is at the task floor, one member is already saturated, or the members fail on the same subtasks, arbitration typically does no better than the best policy.
% Finally, arbitration adds test-time computation. With $k{=}c$, the cost is constant at $ME$ model and value evaluations per environment step, which amounts to a few milliseconds on CPU (\cref{app:runtime}), a modest overhead. This overhead also suggests a direction for future work: the same world model can be used offline, either as a teacher that distills the arbiter's choices into a single policy or as a DAgger-style generator of corrected training data, retaining the reliability gained through arbitration without a world model in the inference loop.
Finally, arbitration adds a few milliseconds of computation per step (\cref{app:runtime}); future work could remove it by using the world model offline, either to distill the arbiter's choices into a single policy or to generate corrected training data in the style of DAgger.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
% We have introduced World-Model Policy Arbiter (\wmpa), a test-time framework augmenting frozen goal-conditioned policies with learned policy selection. \wmpa{} rolls each policy forward in a learned world model, ranks the imagined futures with a single shared goal-conditioned value function, and executes the highest-scoring controller for a short commitment interval. It requires neither policy retraining nor a privileged task-specific progress signal. Under the official OGBench protocol, the arbitrated portfolio improves upon the best policy in its bank by \MeanGain{} points on average and by $33$--$41$ points on multi-object manipulation, is significantly above a per-episode hindsight oracle on \NumAboveUnionSig{} datasets, and outperforms a random-switching baseline. More broadly, these results motivate viewing world models as test-time evaluators of existing goal-conditioned behaviors, not only as generators of new actions.

We introduced World-Model Policy Arbiter (\wmpa{}), which selects among frozen goal-conditioned policies at test time by rolling each forward in a learned world model, scoring the imagined futures with one shared value function, and executing the best policy for a short commitment interval. Without retraining any policy, it improves on the best policy in its bank by \MeanGain{} points on average and by 33--41 points on multi-object manipulation, and it is significantly above a per-episode hindsight oracle on \NumAboveUnionSig{} datasets. These results suggest viewing world models as test-time evaluators of existing behaviors, not only as generators of new actions.

\Cref{tab:stall} reports the results of the stall-restart controller, a model-free baseline that switches policies in response to \emph{evidence of being stuck} rather than on a fixed schedule. The controller draws the first
policy uniformly at random from the bank and then continues to execute the incumbent policy. At every step, it measures the displacement of the state over the last $m$ environment steps, $\|\nu(s_t)-\nu(s_{t-m})\|_2$, in the
normalized observation space that is also used by the world model. In this space, each dimension is divided by its standard deviation over the dataset, so that the threshold is dimensionless and identical across datasets. When

\Cref{tab:value-head-selection} presents a post hoc diagnostic analysis of value-head ranking quality; this analysis did not influence the value-head choices reported in \cref{tab:main}. We assess each head by measuring how closely its ranking of states agrees with the observed time to reach a shared goal in offline trajectories. We draw a state $s_a$ and a later state $g$ from one trajectory, and a state $s_b$ from a second trajectory that also reaches $g$ under the success predicate of the environment; a head is counted as concordant when it assigns the higher value $V(\cdot,g)$ to the state with the shorter observed time to reach $g$. Taken together, these results suggest that this criterion can guide the selection of the value head on new datasets for which a goal-attainment predicate is available.

\section{World-Model Architecture and Training Details}
\label[appendix]{app:wm}

\paragraph{Data.} Each auxiliary model is trained on the OGBench training split of its dataset, with the benchmark's validation split used only for monitoring. Observation and one-step-delta statistics ($\mu_s,\sigma_s,\mu_\Delta,\sigma_\Delta$) are computed once from valid training transitions and stored in the checkpoint.

\paragraph{Dynamics ensemble.} $E=3$ members with disjoint parameters; each is an MLP with three hidden layers of width $512$, GELU activations, and layer normalization, mapping the normalized state and action to a normalized delta of the full observation vector (observation dimension $28$--$115$ depending on the dataset). Training unrolls each member for $H$ steps ($H=5$ for cube, scene, and puzzle; $H=10$ for the two mazes) with a curriculum that increases the unrolled horizon from $1$ to $H$ over the first $10^5$ steps. Members share the training batches and are diversified only by independent parameter initialization (and by unrolling their own predictions); at arbitration time value head $e$ scores the rollout of dynamics member $e$, and the ensemble is reduced by the mean over member pairs.

\paragraph{Metric value head.} For each ensemble member, a goal representation $\psi$ (an MLP $512\times3\rightarrow10$, length-normalized to $\sqrt{10}$) and state and goal encoders $\varphi_S,\varphi_G$ (each an MLP $512\times3\rightarrow64$) define $V=-\|\varphi_S(s)-\varphi_G(\psi(g))\|_2$, following LAVL \citep{kang2026lavl}; target networks are Polyak-averaged with $\tau=0.005$, and goals are relabeled with the OGBench value-goal mixture ($0.2$ current, $0.5$ geometric future, $0.3$ random). The per-family $\gamma$, $\kappa$, and $\lambda_{\mathrm{smooth}}$ are listed in \cref{tab:settings}. Where $\lambda_{\mathrm{smooth}}>0$ (mazes and scene), the local-smoothness regularizer softly bounds the one-step change of the value toward a random goal $\tilde g$,
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{smooth}}
= \E\Big[0.1\,\mathrm{softplus}\Big(10\big[\big(V_{\phi_e}(s',\tilde g)-V_{\phi_e}(s,\tilde g)\big)^2-\tau_V^2\big]\Big)\Big],
\qquad
\tau_V = 1-(1-\gamma)\,\E[\bar V],
\label{eq:smooth}
\end{equation}
where $\E[\bar V]$ is an exponential moving average of the mean value; this term is added to \cref{eq:expectile} with weight $\lambda_{\mathrm{smooth}}$. The expectile target is the implicit-maximization heuristic of IQL-style value learning \citep{kostrikov2022iql,park2023hiql}; under stochastic transitions, it is also optimistic with respect to next-state noise, a bias of the scorer that pessimistic ensemble reductions alter macro-average success by at most one point (\cref{app:scoreabl}).

\paragraph{Direct value head (puzzle).} The direct value $V_\theta$ follows the design of GCIQL, namely an MLP with three hidden layers of width $512$ and layer normalization, and is trained with the puzzle recipe of the reference implementation: the same relabeling mixture, $\gamma=0.99$, $\tau=0.005$, $\kappa=0.9$, and no value or target clipping. The two action-value heads $Q_{\omega,1},Q_{\omega,2}$ share this architecture and the training batches; $\bar Q$ in \cref{eq:direct-expectile} is the minimum of their target copies, and the same $\min_j Q_{\omega,j}$ is the scorer of the model-free Q-select baseline (\cref{tab:scorer-selector}).

\paragraph{Optimization.} The dynamics and value losses are summed with unit weights and optimized with Adam (learning rate $3\times10^{-4}$) at batch size $1024$ for $10^6$ steps on a single H100 MIG slice. The checkpoint at $10^6$ steps is used throughout. The same code path serves both training and test-time imagination, so that predicted states can be consumed directly by every frozen policy. The joint optimization described here applies to the dynamics and metric-value networks. The direct scorer is loaded from the pretrained GCIQL checkpoint and remains frozen during model training.

\section{When Do Imagined Rollouts Support Useful Arbitration?}
\label[appendix]{app:wmpa_theory}

Fix a commitment interval $c$, and let $\pi_b$ be the fixed bank member
with the highest success probability under the evaluation distribution.
Write $J(\pi)$ for the probability that policy $\pi$ reaches the goal by
the deadline $T$. A decision state $x$ includes the environment state,
goal, remaining time, and success or termination status. Success and
termination are absorbing, and the final commitment interval is shortened
to the remaining episode length. There are $N=\lceil T/c\rceil$ decision
slots, with absorbing trajectories padded to the deadline.

The \emph{chunk advantage} of member $i$ relative to $\pi_b$ is
\begin{equation}
    A_i(x)
    = \Pr\!\left(\text{success}\mid x,\
        \pi_i\text{ for one chunk, then }\pi_b\right)
    - \Pr\!\left(\text{success}\mid x,\
        \pi_b\text{ throughout}\right).
    \label{eq:wmpa_chunk_advantage}
\end{equation}
This quantity measures the benefit of using member $i$ for the next
chunk while holding the subsequent controller fixed. Since $A_b(x)=0$,
the largest available advantage,
\begin{equation}
    G(x)=\max_i A_i(x)\geq 0,
    \label{eq:wmpa_opportunity}
\end{equation}
measures the opportunity to improve on the best fixed member at that
decision point.

Let $S_i(x)$ be the imagined-rollout score used by \wmpa{}, and let
$S_i^\star(x)$ be the expected value of the same complete scoring rule
under the true dynamics, with the learned value head unchanged. In
particular, the expectation is taken after applying the rule's
aggregation over ensemble members and maximum over rollout steps. Any
sampling error in the computed score $S_i(x)$ is included in the
discrepancy below. Define
\begin{equation}
    e(x)=\max_i\left|S_i(x)-S_i^\star(x)\right|.
    \label{eq:wmpa_score_discrepancy}
\end{equation}
Thus, $e(x)$ measures the largest discrepancy between the computed and
true-dynamics scores across the bank. After success or termination, set all
advantages and scores to zero.

\begin{wmpaassumption}[Separation of inferior choices]
\label{ass:wmpa_score_separation}
For some tolerance $0\leq\delta<1$ and margin $\Delta>0$, at every
active decision state visited by \wmpa{},
\begin{equation}
    A_i(x)<G(x)-\delta
    \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
    S_i^\star(x)\leq\max_j S_j^\star(x)-\Delta.
    \label{eq:wmpa_score_separation}
\end{equation}
\end{wmpaassumption}

The assumption requires the value head, when applied to true-dynamics
rollouts, to separate any member whose advantage is more than $\delta$
below the best by a score margin of at least $\Delta$. It does not require
correct ordering among members whose advantages are within $\delta$ of
the best. The tolerance is measured in success-probability units, whereas
the margin is measured in the scorer's own units.

\begin{wmpatheorem}[Improvement through robust selection]
\label{thm:wmpa_robust_selection}
Under Assumption~\ref{ass:wmpa_score_separation}, let $X_n$ be the decision
state at slot $n$ under \wmpa{}, and let $\mathcal A_n$ be the event that
$X_n$ is active. Then
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
    J(\pi_{\mathrm{WMPA}})-J(\pi_b)
    \geq \sum_{n=0}^{N-1}
    \biggl(&\mathbb E_{\mathrm{WMPA}}[G(X_n)]
    -\delta\Pr_{\mathrm{WMPA}}(\mathcal A_n)\\
    &-(1-\delta)\Pr_{\mathrm{WMPA}}\!\left(
        \mathcal A_n,\,
        e(X_n)\geq\frac{\Delta}{2}\right)\biggr).
\end{split}
\label{eq:wmpa_robust_improvement}
\end{equation}
In particular, a positive right-hand side guarantees improvement over
every fixed bank member.
\end{wmpatheorem}

The bound compares the available improvement with two sources of
selection loss at active decision slots: the tolerated difference $\delta$
from the best chunk advantage, and decisions at which score error is
large enough to overturn the separation margin. Errors smaller than half
the margin cannot cause a selection more than $\delta$ below the best
advantage.

\begin{proof}
At an active decision state $x$, let $\hat i$ maximize the computed score
and let $j$ maximize the true-dynamics score. Since
$S_{\hat i}(x)\geq S_j(x)$,
\begin{equation}
    S_j^\star(x)-S_{\hat i}^\star(x)\leq 2e(x).
    \label{eq:wmpa_margin_stability}
\end{equation}
If $e(x)<\Delta/2$, Assumption~\ref{ass:wmpa_score_separation} implies
$A_{\hat i}(x)\geq G(x)-\delta$. Otherwise,
$G(x)-A_{\hat i}(x)\leq 1$, since the common reference term cancels and
the remaining difference is between two success probabilities.
At an absorbing decision state, all advantages are zero. Consequently,
for every decision state $x$,
\begin{equation}
    A_{\hat i}(x)
    \geq G(x)-\delta\mathbf{1}\{x\text{ is active}\}
    -(1-\delta)\mathbf{1}\!\left\{
        x\text{ is active},\ e(x)\geq\frac{\Delta}{2}\right\}.
    \label{eq:wmpa_local_selection_bound}
\end{equation}

Let $V_b(x)$ denote the probability of success if $\pi_b$ is followed
from $x$ onward. By definition, the expected change in $V_b$ across a
chunk executed by member $i$ is $A_i(x)$. Summing these changes along
\wmpa{}'s trajectory cancels the intermediate reference values. At the
deadline, $V_b$ equals the success indicator; its expectation at the
initial state equals $J(\pi_b)$. Therefore,
\begin{equation}
    J(\pi_{\mathrm{WMPA}})-J(\pi_b)
    =\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}
    \mathbb E_{\mathrm{WMPA}}[A_{\hat i_n}(X_n)],
    \label{eq:wmpa_advantage_identity}
\end{equation}
where $\hat i_n$ is the member selected at slot $n$. Taking expectations
in~\eqref{eq:wmpa_local_selection_bound} and summing proves the result.
\end{proof}

The result identifies three requirements for useful arbitration: the bank
must offer locally advantageous alternatives, the value head must
separate substantially inferior choices under true dynamics, and score
errors must be small relative to that separation. Accurate dynamics
alone are insufficient if the value head favors unhelpful outcomes.
Conversely, imperfect imagined rollouts can support reliable selection
when their score errors preserve the relevant margin.
