\begin{abstract}
Offline goal-conditioned reinforcement learning (GCRL) has produced a diverse set of goal-reaching algorithms, yet no single algorithm performs best across environments, goals, and even different phases of the same task. The standard practice is to evaluate a pool of candidate policies, retain the one that performs best in a given environment, and discard the rest. We instead ask whether frozen goal-conditioned policies can be retained and used collectively as a \emph{portfolio}. This setting poses two key challenges: the native critics associated with different policies are either not directly comparable or entirely absent, and uninformed switching between closed-loop controllers can disrupt coherent behavior. We introduce World-Model Policy Arbiter (\wmpa), a test-time framework that rolls each frozen policy forward in a learned state-space world model, evaluates the imagined futures with a shared goal-conditioned value function, and executes the highest-scoring policy for a short commitment interval before the next round of arbitration. \wmpa{} requires neither policy retraining nor privileged task-specific knowledge. Under the official OGBench evaluation protocol on \NumEnvs{} state-based datasets spanning maze navigation as well as cube, scene, and puzzle manipulation, \wmpa{} improves the macro-average success rate from the \MeanBest\% achieved by the best policy selected per dataset to \MeanWMPP\%, with statistically significant gains on \NumSigUp{} datasets. These gains include \GainCubeDoublePlay{} percentage points on \texttt{cube-double-play} and \GainScenePlay{} percentage points on \texttt{scene-play}.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Goal-conditioned reinforcement learning (GCRL) seeks to learn policies that reach user-specified goals. A wide range of approaches has been developed for this setting, including behavior cloning, temporal-difference learning, contrastive learning, quasimetric learning, and hierarchical control \citep{eysenbach2022contrastive,wang2023quasimetric,park2023hiql}. Because these approaches use different algorithmic structures, rely on different assumptions, and induce different behaviors, their relative performance varies across environments, goals, and task horizons; no single method consistently dominates \citep{park2025ogbench}.

Most prior work focuses on training a stronger individual policy. Offline GCRL, however, naturally raises a different question. The standard practice evaluates these policies, retains the best-performing one, and discards the remainder. This procedure overlooks behaviors of the discarded policies that are useful only in particular states or phases of a task, and it leaves a basic question unanswered: \emph{given a set of frozen goal-conditioned policies, which policy should act from the current state for the current goal?} This selection problem is challenging for three reasons. First, the critics and learned representations of independently trained policies are not directly comparable, and some methods do not learn a value function at all. Second, each policy is a closed-loop controller, so its first action provides little information about the trajectory it will induce. Third, unconstrained switching between policies can disrupt temporally coherent behavior.

Our key idea is to compare policies through the consequences they are predicted to produce. From the current state, our method, World-Model Policy Arbiter (\wmpa), rolls each frozen policy forward in a learned world model while conditioning all policies on the same final goal. It scores the resulting imagined futures with a shared goal-conditioned value function, executes the highest-scoring policy for a fixed commitment interval, and repeats this procedure. Unlike action-level model-predictive control, \wmpa{} does not optimize new action sequences; instead, it evaluates the behaviors already represented in the policy bank. All base policies remain frozen, and the selector requires no hand-designed progress signal, no rollouts in the real environment, and no privileged task knowledge.

Scoring imagined states introduces an additional challenge because model rollouts produce state--goal pairs that are underrepresented in the training data.
By default, we address this issue with a metric value function that uses separate state and goal encoders and defines the score as the negative Euclidean distance between the latent representations of the state and the goal.
The resulting latent geometry provides a structured score for imagined pairs, and the separate encoders preserve the directionality of reachability \citep{kang2026lavl}.
We select the scoring method at the level of dataset families.
We hypothesize that, in the puzzle datasets, task progress is represented by a combinatorial button pattern that latent distance does not order reliably.
For these datasets, we therefore score the same imagined futures with a direct goal-conditioned value network instead (\cref{sec:setup}).

We make the following contributions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Policy arbitration for GCRL.} We formulate test-time control over a bank of independently trained goal-conditioned policies as a policy-selection problem, which is distinct from selecting a single global winner or retargeting the subgoal of a single controller. We argue that offline GCRL is the natural setting for this problem: the policy bank incurs no additional data collection cost, because its members are trained entirely from the offline dataset without online interaction; moreover, goal-conditioning multiplies the situations in which different members excel.
    \item \textbf{A novel method for policy arbitration.} \wmpa{} compares frozen policies by scoring their closed-loop imagined futures with a shared goal-conditioned value function, without policy retraining or privileged signals. We set the scoring horizon equal to the commitment interval ($k=c$) so that the amortized cost of arbitration per environment step remains constant, and the arbiter adds $3$--$4$\,ms per step on CPU.
    \item \textbf{Broad empirical study.} We evaluate \wmpa{} on \NumEnvs{} state-based OGBench datasets under the official protocol. We compare against every policy in the bank, a random-switching baseline with a matched commitment interval, and additional model-free and model-based baselines. We further analyze when arbitration is beneficial, using a per-episode hindsight oracle and policy-bank statistics.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Goal-conditioned reinforcement learning.}
GCRL learns policies conditioned on a desired goal, using supervised, value-based, contrastive, quasimetric, and hierarchical objectives \citep{eysenbach2022contrastive,wang2023quasimetric,park2023hiql}; OGBench provides common datasets and reference implementations, and it shows that the strengths of these methods vary across navigation, stitching, stochasticity, and manipulation \citep{park2025ogbench}. Model-based GCRL uses a model to improve the policy being trained: planning with a learned model to fine-tune offline policies \citep[GOPlan;][]{wang2024goplan}, planning exploration goals \citep[PEG;][]{hu2023peg}, modeling transitions between arbitrary buffer states for goal navigation \citep{duan2024mun}, synthesizing goal-reaching data backward \citep{hoftmann2023backward}, or constructing self-curricula \citep{gong2024gcpo}; see \citet{liu2022gcrl} for a survey. Stronger recent learners \citep{dong2026hiflowq} and abstraction-based state--goal representations \citep{wibault2026abstraction} are complementary to \wmpa{}: they could serve as additional bank members and can supply the value head. Goal-conditioned hypernetworks even generate policies on demand \citep{faccio2023goalcond}. All of these approaches modify or create the policy; we leave every policy frozen and use the model only to choose among them.

\paragraph{Test-time planning with frozen policies and world models.}
Existing test-time methods devote computation to \emph{producing} behavior. Goal-conditioned policies serve as low-level controllers for planning through intermediate goals \citep{nasiriany2019planning}; Test-Time Graph Search builds a graph over dataset states and feeds subgoals to a single frozen policy \citep{opryshko2026ttgs}; CompPlan composes subgoal-conditioned instances of a single policy family through policy-conditioned jumpy models \citep{farebrother2026compplan}; and ALPS plans over Laplacian subgoal clusters with a one-step model \citep{shehmar2026laplacian}. All these methods ask \emph{which subgoal should one controller pursue?}, whereas \wmpa{} keeps the final goal fixed and asks \emph{which controller should act now?}, so we believe they are complementary. Hand-offs between task and recovery policies in safety-critical offline GCRL \citep{cao2024rbsl} are a two-controller special case of the switching \wmpa{} performs over an arbitrary bank with a learned, model-based trigger. World models, in turn, are used for action-level planning or training an amortized policy \citep{schrittwieser2020muzero,hafner2023dreamerv3}; sampling-based MPC with learned models \citep{williams2017mppi,hansen2022tdmpc} is the action-level baseline of \cref{app:ablations}, and hierarchical or reset-free world models \citep{schiewer2024hierarchical,yang2024resetfree} likewise generate behavior rather than evaluate it. \wmpa{} uses the model only to evaluate closed-loop behaviors already in the bank.

\paragraph{Policy switching, options, and learned hierarchies.}
Selecting among fixed policies by simulating each is a long-standing idea: policy switching and parallel rollout execute, at every state, the base policy with the highest simulated return, and both provably improve upon every base policy \citep{chang2004parallel,bertsekas1999rollout}; generalized policy improvement (GPI) achieves the same result with per-policy action-values, provided these values are comparable \citep{barreto2017successor}. In the options framework, each frozen member of $\mathbf{\Pi}$ is an option with a fixed-duration termination condition, and re-arbitrating every $c$ steps is a scheduled form of option interruption, which is guaranteed not to degrade performance when the switching value is exact \citep{sutton1999between}. \wmpa{} instantiates this lineage with a learned model, a truncated horizon, and goal conditioning: the model supplies the rollouts that the classical methods obtain from a simulator, and the shared value function avoids the need for the comparable per-policy action-value estimates required by GPI.
Model-based hierarchical RL is a distinct lineage. Director selects latent goals for a worker trained in imagination \citep{hafner2022director}; SkiMo plans with a skill-dynamics model learned jointly with a skill VAE \citep{shi2022skimo}; SPlaTES shapes skills to be predictable by an abstract world model and plans over them with iCEM \citep{gurtler2025splates}; Puppeteer trains a separate high-level world model for each task on top of a frozen tracking controller \citep{hansen2025puppeteer}; and a learned gate over a frozen planner decides how much search to perform \citep{muppidi2026finding}. All of these methods learn the high level, most also co-adapt the low level, and their models generate or search over the executed behavior. \wmpa{} learns neither level: the candidates are complete goal-conditioned policies trained independently with different algorithms, the dynamics model is trained once on the dataset without reference to any policy, and the model only arbitrates among the candidates.

\section{Goal-Conditioned Reinforcement Learning Preliminaries}

Following \citet{park2025ogbench}, we consider goal-conditioned reinforcement learning over a controlled Markov process
$\mathcal{M}=(\calS,\calA,\calG,\mu,p)$.
Here, $\calS$ is the state space, $\calA$ is the action space, $\calG$ is the goal space, $\mu\in\Delta(\calS)$ is the initial-state distribution, and $p(s'\mid s,a)\colon\calS\times\calA\to\Delta(\calS)$ denotes the transition dynamics, where $\Delta(\cdot)$ denotes the space of probability distributions over its argument. A goal-conditioned policy $\pi(a\mid s,g)\colon\calS\times\calG\to\Delta(\calA)$ maps the current state and goal to a distribution over actions.

Let $\nu\in\Delta(\calS\times\calG)$ denote the evaluation distribution over initial-state and goal pairs. Given $(s_0,g)\sim\nu$, a policy induces a trajectory $\tau=(s_0,a_0,s_1,\ldots,a_{T-1},s_T)$ according to the transition dynamics. Its performance is measured by expected goal-reaching success:
\[
    J(\pi)
    =
    \mathbb{E}_{(s_0,g)\sim\nu,\,
    \tau\sim p^\pi(\tau\mid s_0,g)}
    \left[\operatorname{Succ}(\tau,g)\right],
\]
where $\operatorname{Succ}(\tau,g)\in\{0,1\}$ indicates whether $\tau$ reaches $g$ within the evaluation horizon $T$ according to the success criterion of the environment. This success criterion is used only for evaluation and is not available during training. Instead, goal-reaching supervision is constructed through hindsight goal relabeling, using states from logged trajectories as goals.

We assume access to a logged trajectory dataset $\calD=\{\tau^{(n)}\}_{n=1}^N$, where each trajectory $\tau^{(n)}=(s_0^{(n)},a_0^{(n)},s_1^{(n)},\ldots,s_{T_n}^{(n)})$ is collected as part of an offline dataset. We use $\calD$ to train the auxiliary world model and value function. In our experiments, $\calD$ is the OGBench training split, and all policies are trained offline using the same split. The general formulation, however, does not require the policies to be trained offline or from a shared dataset.

Rather than learning a single goal-conditioned policy, we are given a portfolio of $M$ frozen policies,
\[
    \PiBank=\{\pi_i(a\mid s,g)\}_{i=1}^M,
\]
whose members may differ in the GCRL algorithm or random seed used for training. All members share a common state, action, and goal interface, and the arbiter requires only black-box access to their action distributions. Internal value estimates and representations are neither assumed to be accessible nor required to be comparable across policies.

At arbitration time $t$, the arbiter selects a policy index $z_t\in\{1,\ldots,M\}$. The selected policy then controls the agent for a commitment interval of length $c$:
\[
    a_{t+j}\sim\pi_{z_t}(\cdot\mid s_{t+j},g),
    \qquad j=0,\ldots,c-1.
\]
The resulting controller is therefore a switching policy over the frozen portfolio members. The policy-portfolio objective is to maximize the expected goal-reaching success of this induced policy without modifying any member of $\PiBank$. The central problem is thus to determine, from the current state and goal, which portfolio member is expected to produce the most favorable future trajectory.
\section{World-Model Policy Arbiter}
\label{sec:method}
\wmpa{} has three components (\cref{fig:overview}): a bank of frozen goal-conditioned policies, a learned state-space world model for rolling every policy forward, and a single goal-conditioned value function that scores all policies' imagined futures. At each arbitration point the arbiter imagines $k$ steps of every policy, ranks candidates by the value of their imagined states, and executes the winner for $c=k$ environment steps before arbitrating again. The result is a switching controller over the bank: \cref{fig:episode} shows an episode in which the arbiter alternates among policies. The three subsections below describe the world model, the value function, and the rollout and ranking step.

\subsection{Learned state-space world model}

For each environment, we train an ensemble of $E$ independently parameterized MLP dynamics models~\citep{kurutach2018model} directly in the benchmark state space. Throughout this section, all states are raw benchmark states; the fixed normalizer $\nu(s)=(s-\mu_s)/\sigma_s$, whose statistics are computed from the logged trajectories, is applied only at the network input. Each member predicts a normalized one-step delta and returns the next state in raw coordinates,
\begin{align}
\hat{\delta}^{(e)}_t &= f_{\theta_e}\big(\nu(s_t),a_t\big),\\
\hat{s}^{(e)}_{t+1} &= \hat f_{\theta_e}(s_t,a_t) := s_t + \mu_{\Delta}+\sigma_{\Delta}\hat{\delta}^{(e)}_t,
\end{align}
where $\mu_{\Delta}$ and $\sigma_{\Delta}$ are fixed statistics computed from valid logged transitions. Multi-step predictions are obtained by applying the same raw-state map $\hat f_{\theta_e}$ to its own outputs, with re-normalization at every input. The outputs are trained to match non-normalized environment states and are consumed by the frozen policies exactly like real observations. The members are trained on $H$-step windows by unrolling their own predictions and penalizing the normalized error at every step,
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{dyn}} = \frac{1}{E}\sum_{e=1}^{E}
\E_{\tau\sim\calD}
\left[\sum_{h=1}^{H} w_h\left\|\nu\big(\hat{s}^{(e)}_{t+h}\big)-\nu\big(s_{t+h}\big)\right\|_2^2\right],
\end{equation}
with a curriculum that increases the effective horizon from $1$ to $H$ over the first $10^5$ updates ($w_h$ uniform over the active steps). Predicting in state space isolates arbitration from representation learning and guarantees that imagined states are valid inputs to every frozen policy.

\subsection{Goal-conditioned value function}
\label{sec:value}

The arbiter scores every candidate's imagined states with a single goal-conditioned value function $V(s,g)$, so policies trained with different objectives are compared on a common scale. Only the ordering matters: futures reached by different candidates from the same decision state should be ranked in the same order as their realized outcomes. Nothing in the arbiter depends on how $V$ is parameterized, and we use two parameterizations whose inductive biases match different kinds of task progress; we choose between them per task family (\cref{sec:setup}, \cref{app:hyperparams}). We also swap the two value heads to show the performance difference (\cref{tab:scorer-selector}).

\paragraph{Metric value (maze, cube, scene).} Where progress is geometric, like moving through a maze, displacing objects toward target poses, we use a latent distance with separate state and goal encoders for each ensemble member,
\begin{equation}
V_{\phi_e}(s,g)
= -\left\|\varphi^{(e)}_{S}(s)-\varphi^{(e)}_{G}(\psi^{(e)}(g))\right\|_2,
\label{eq:metric-value}
\end{equation}
where $\psi^{(e)}$ is a low-dimensional goal representation, following \citet{kang2026lavl}. The distance orders imagined state--goal pairs even where closed-loop rollouts leave the support of the logged data, and the distinct encoders keep reachability directional ($V_{\phi_e}(s,g)\neq V_{\phi_e}(g,s)$ in general). Each value member is trained jointly with its corresponding dynamics member by an expectile temporal-difference objective on relabeled goals with a sparse reward ($0$ at attainment and $-1$ otherwise),
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{value}}
&= \frac{1}{E}\sum_{e=1}^{E}
\E\!\left[
\left|\kappa-\mathbf{1}\{\bar\delta_e<0\}\right|
\big(y_e-V_{\phi_e}(s,g)\big)^2
\right], \\
y_e
&= r(s,g)+\gamma\,m(s,g)\,
\bar V_{\bar\phi_e}(s',g).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:expectile}
\end{equation}
where $\bar V$ is a target network, $m(s,g)=0$ at attainment, $\bar\delta_e=y_e-\bar V_{\bar\phi_e}(s,g)$, and the expectile satisfies $\kappa>0.5$ (settings in \cref{app:wm}).

\paragraph{Direct value (puzzle).} On the puzzle datasets progress is combinatorial: a state is a pattern of buttons, a press toggles a button together with its neighbours, and the number of presses to the goal pattern is not monotone in embedding distance. Thus, we use a value with no geometric structure,
\begin{equation}
V_{\theta}(s,g) = \mathrm{MLP}_{\theta}\big([\,s;\,g\,]\big),
\label{eq:direct-value}
\end{equation}
trained by implicit Q-learning \citep{kostrikov2022iql} on the same dataset. Two action-value heads $Q_{\omega,i}(s,a,g)$ are regressed toward the one-step target $r(s,g)+\gamma\,m(s,g)\,V_{\theta}(s',g)$, and the state value is the $\kappa$-expectile of the target critic $\bar Q=\min_i \bar Q_{\omega,i}$ under the dataset's actions,
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{V}
= \E\Big[\left|\kappa-\mathbf{1}\{\bar Q(s,a,g)-V_{\theta}(s,g)<0\}\right|\big(\bar Q(s,a,g)-V_{\theta}(s,g)\big)^2\Big],
\label{eq:direct-expectile}
\end{equation}
so that $V_{\theta}$ approximates the value of the best dataset-supported action at each relabeled state--goal pair. It is the state-value network of the bank's IQL member, trained with the reference implementation's settings and reused unchanged as the scorer. Being one function applied to every candidate's imagined states, it keeps scores comparable across the bank exactly as the metric value does.

\subsection{Counterfactual rollout and ranking}

At each arbitration point, every frozen policy is rolled out in closed loop for $k$ model steps, acting deterministically on its own imagined states. All policies in the bank are Gaussian actors whose deterministic mode is the behavior deployed and evaluated by the benchmark, so the imagined branch reproduces the behavior that would be executed; we do not evaluate stochastic rollouts obtained by sampling actions from the policy distributions. For policy $i$ and ensemble member $e$,
\begin{align}
\hat{s}^{i,e}_{0} &= s_t,\\
\hat{a}^{i,e}_{h} &= \pi_i(\hat{s}^{i,e}_{h},g),\\
\hat{s}^{i,e}_{h+1} &= \hat{f}_{\theta_e}(\hat{s}^{i,e}_{h},\hat{a}^{i,e}_{h}),
\qquad h=0,\ldots,k-1,
\end{align}
where $\hat f_{\theta_e}$ is transition model ensemble member of \cref{sec:method}.
We score each candidate by the highest value imagined state along the rollout,
\begin{equation}
S_i(s_t,g)=\max_{1\le h\le k}
\frac{1}{E}\sum_{e=1}^{E}
V_{\phi_e}(\hat{s}^{i,e}_{h},g).
\label{eq:score}
\end{equation}
The max operator allows a policy to receive credit for reaching the goal before the end of the planning horizon. \cref{app:scoreabl} ablates both reductions (mean and final-state alternatives over the horizon; min and mean$-$std over ensemble members). \wmpa{} selects $i^*=\arg\max_i S_i$ and executes $\pi_{i^*}$ in the real environment for $c$ steps, and then repeats the arbitration (\cref{alg:wmpp}, \cref{app:hyperparams}).

\paragraph{Rollout length and policy commitment steps.}
A single arbitration costs $M\cdot E\cdot k$ dynamics evaluations and the same
number of value evaluations, where $M$ is the number of policies in the bank
and $E$ is the number of members of the dynamics ensemble ($M=6$ and $E=3$
throughout, \cref{sec:setup,app:wm}). Amortized over the commitment interval, the nominal model budget per environment step is $M E k/c$. We always set the two horizons equal, $k=c$, so that every configuration incurs the same cost of $M E$ model calls per environment step. The horizon is therefore a single hyperparameter that trades short-horizon reactivity against long-horizon foresight with no difference in amortized computational cost; it is fixed per task family (\cref{sec:setup}, \cref{app:hyperparams}).
\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:setup}

We evaluate \wmpa{} on \NumEnvs{} state-based OGBench datasets \citep{park2025ogbench}: two maze-navigation datasets (\texttt{pointmaze-medium} and \texttt{antmaze-large}) as well as six cube (\texttt{single} to \texttt{triple}), two scene, and eight puzzle (\texttt{3x3} to \texttt{4x6}) manipulation datasets, which together cover the \texttt{play} and \texttt{noisy} data-collection variants (\cref{app:hyperparams}). OGBench exposes the same observation, action, goal, and evaluation interfaces to policies trained with different objectives, and the use of state observations isolates arbitration from visual representation learning. The portfolio contains the six OGBench reference learners (GCBC, GCIVL, GCIQL, QRL, CRL, and HIQL), each trained with the unmodified reference implementation and per-family hyperparameters for $10^6$ steps. The policy bank holds one checkpoint per algorithm, and the study is replicated over three bank seeds. All policies remain frozen; the scores of the individual members are reported in \cref{tab:main}. All methods are evaluated on the same fixed episode seeds, so every comparison is paired per episode; reported intervals are 95\% hierarchical bootstraps over bank seeds and then episodes, and a gain is called significant when the interval of the paired difference excludes zero (\cref{app:bank-stats}).

\subsection{Does the world model matter?}
\label{sec:ablation}

We study a \randomswitch{} ablation which removes the entire learned model: it uses neither the world-model rollouts nor the value head, and draws a policy uniformly from the bank at every arbitration point. Everything else, including the bank, the commitment interval, and the evaluation episodes, is shared with \wmpa{}, so the comparison isolates what the learned scoring of imagined futures contributes over the switching schedule alone. Under per-episode pairing, \wmpa{} outperforms \randomswitch{} by \MeanWMPPminusRandom{} points on average, with significant gains on \NumWMPPvsRandomSig{} datasets. Random switching alone is significantly below the best policy on \NumRandomSigDown{} datasets and significantly above it on \NumRandomSigUp{}. \wmpa{}'s major gains therefore come neither from the commitment schedule nor from cycling through policies, but from model-based candidate ranking. Full results of this ablation are presented in \cref{tab:random-c}.

A stronger model-free baseline retains the selected policy and switches to a randomly chosen other policy only when the agent stalls. Stalling is measured as the net displacement of the full observation vector over the last $m$ steps, $\|\nu(s_t)-\nu(s_{t-m})\|_2$, in the standardized space of \cref{sec:method}, so the threshold $\varepsilon=0.05$ is dimensionless and is fixed across all datasets without tuning. \Cref{app:random-c} evaluates this heuristic for $m\in\{5,10,25,50\}$: it does not recover the manipulation gains, meaning a displacement trigger is a poor proxy for the situations in which switching is beneficial. Full results of this ablation are presented in \cref{tab:stall}.

Because the oracle already selects a successful policy for each evaluation episode whenever one exists, per-episode selection alone cannot account for these gains; instead, they indicate a benefit of composing policies \emph{within} an episode (\cref{fig:episode} shows one such episode).
Second, neither a large gap between the best and the second-best policy nor limited headroom between the best policy and the oracle precludes gains from switching.
On \texttt{puzzle-3x3-play}, \wmpa{} improves over the best policy by \GainPuzzleThreebyThreePlay{} percentage points, even though the success rate of the second-best policy is \DgapPuzzleThreebyThreePlay{} percentage points lower than that of the best policy and the headroom to the per-episode oracle is only \HeadroomPuzzleThreebyThreePlay{}\%.

Both gains exceed the corresponding static headroom, which shows that within-episode composition can improve performance even when a single member is substantially stronger than the others overall.
Moreover, the bank need not be drawn from a single dataset: pooling the policies trained on the \texttt{play} and \texttt{noisy} data of an environment into one twelve-member bank never degrades the performance of \wmpa{} and improves it whenever the added members contribute new competencies (\cref{app:bank-composition}).

\section{Limitations and Future Work}
% \label{sec:limitations}
% % only use state-based datasets for now; compute overhead during test-time; failure mode ceiling cases, no complementary failures. future work could be using wm to distill abilities and as dagger to create more reliable offline data to mitigate the need of using wm during inference time so to avoid the compute overhead
% The study uses state observations; pixels would require a policy-compatible latent world model or a shared visual encoder. The auxiliary models are trained per environment, so the contribution is policy reuse, not cross-environment transfer, and the metric architecture is an inductive bias, not a guarantee, on imagined states. The per-family interval and value parameterization were fixed on held-out episodes of the same five evaluation goals; OGBench provides no further goals, so their transfer to unseen goals is untested. Random switching with a per-dataset post-hoc interval remains competitive on a few datasets where stalling policies benefit from any restart, and with a scorer that ranks candidates at chance a single dominant policy makes arbitration a net loss. All bank members are trained on the same dataset with the benchmark's reference implementations; banks that mix data or interfaces are outside the present evidence. Finally, simulation results do not establish robustness on real robots, and the bank-statistic associations rest on \NumEnvs{} datasets.

\label{sec:limitations}
This study uses state observations and per-environment transition models. Pixel observations would require either a latent world model with a shared visual encoder across all policies or a world model that produces realistic images. We leave visual environments to future work. Additionally, \wmpa{} selects among existing behaviors and cannot create new ones: when every member is at the task floor, one member is already saturated, or the members fail on the same episodes, arbitration typically does no better than the best policy.
Finally, arbitration adds test-time computation. With $k{=}c$, the cost is constant at $ME$ model and value evaluations per environment step, which amounts to a few milliseconds on CPU (\cref{app:runtime}), a modest overhead. This overhead also suggests a direction for future work: the same world model can be used offline, either as a teacher that distills the arbiter's choices into a single policy or as a DAgger-style generator of corrected training data, retaining the reliability gained through arbitration without a world model in the inference loop.

\section{Conclusion}

We have introduced World-Model Policy Arbiter (\wmpa), a test-time framework augmenting frozen goal-conditioned policies with learned policy selection. \wmpa{} rolls each policy forward in a learned world model, ranks the imagined futures with a single shared goal-conditioned value function, and executes the highest-scoring controller for a short commitment interval. It requires neither policy retraining nor a privileged task-specific progress signal. Under the official OGBench protocol, the arbitrated portfolio improves upon the best policy in its bank by \MeanGain{} points on average and by $33$--$41$ points on multi-object manipulation, is significantly above a per-episode hindsight oracle on \NumAboveUnionSig{} datasets, and clearly outperforms a random-switching baseline. More broadly, these results motivate viewing world models as test-time evaluators of existing goal-conditioned behaviors, not only as generators of new actions.

\section{Bank Composition}
\label[appendix]{app:bank-composition}

Every bank in the main study is trained on a single dataset. \Cref{tab:bank-composition} tests the next step: for an environment with two datasets, the six policies trained on its \texttt{play} data and the six trained on its \texttt{noisy} data are pooled into a single twelve-member bank, and \wmpa{} arbitrates over all twelve members with the same world model, value function, interval, and episodes as the six-member run on that dataset (nothing is retrained; the extra members are simply loaded from the checkpoints of the other dataset). It isolates how arbitration handles members whose failure modes differ because their training data differ. For each base dataset, the table reports the best policy of the pooled bank, \randomswitch{} and \wmpa{} over the pooled bank, and two paired contrasts: \wmpa{} versus the best policy of the pooled bank, and pooled-bank \wmpa{} versus six-member \wmpa{} on the same episodes. \section{World-Model Architecture and Training Details}
\label[appendix]{app:wm}

\paragraph{Data.} Each auxiliary model is trained on the OGBench training split of its dataset, with the benchmark's validation split used only for monitoring. Observation and one-step-delta statistics ($\mu_s,\sigma_s,\mu_\Delta,\sigma_\Delta$) are computed once from valid training transitions and stored in the checkpoint.

\paragraph{Dynamics ensemble.} $E=3$ members with disjoint parameters; each is an MLP with three hidden layers of width $512$, GELU activations, and layer normalization, mapping the normalized state and action to a normalized delta of the full observation vector (observation dimension $28$--$115$ depending on the dataset). Training unrolls each member for $H$ steps ($H=5$ for cube, scene, and puzzle; $H=10$ for the two mazes) with a curriculum that increases the unrolled horizon from $1$ to $H$ over the first $10^5$ steps. Members share the training batches and are diversified only by independent parameter initialization (and by unrolling their own predictions); at arbitration time value head $e$ scores the rollout of dynamics member $e$, and the ensemble is reduced by the mean over member pairs.

\paragraph{Metric value head.} For each ensemble member, a goal representation $\psi$ (an MLP $512\times3\rightarrow10$, length-normalized to $\sqrt{10}$) and state and goal encoders $\varphi_S,\varphi_G$ (each an MLP $512\times3\rightarrow64$) define $V=-\|\varphi_S(s)-\varphi_G(\psi(g))\|_2$, following LAVL \citep{kang2026lavl}; target networks are Polyak-averaged with $\tau=0.005$, and goals are relabeled with the OGBench value-goal mixture ($0.2$ current, $0.5$ geometric future, $0.3$ random). The per-family $\gamma$, $\kappa$, and $\lambda_{\mathrm{smooth}}$ are listed in \cref{tab:settings}. Where $\lambda_{\mathrm{smooth}}>0$ (mazes and scene), the local-smoothness regularizer softly bounds the one-step change of the value toward a random goal $\tilde g$,
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{smooth}}
= \E\Big[0.1\,\mathrm{softplus}\Big(10\big[\big(V_{\phi_e}(s',\tilde g)-V_{\phi_e}(s,\tilde g)\big)^2-\tau_V^2\big]\Big)\Big],
\qquad
\tau_V = 1-(1-\gamma)\,\E[\bar V],
\label{eq:smooth}
\end{equation}
where $\E[\bar V]$ is an exponential moving average of the mean value; this term is added to \cref{eq:expectile} with weight $\lambda_{\mathrm{smooth}}$. The expectile target is the implicit-maximization heuristic of IQL-style value learning \citep{kostrikov2022iql,park2023hiql}; under stochastic transitions, it is also optimistic with respect to next-state noise, a bias of the scorer that pessimistic ensemble reductions alter macro-average success by at most one point (\cref{app:scoreabl}).

\paragraph{Direct value head (puzzle).} The direct value $V_\theta$ follows the design of GCIQL, namely an MLP with three hidden layers of width $512$ and layer normalization, and is trained with the puzzle recipe of the reference implementation: the same relabeling mixture, $\gamma=0.99$, $\tau=0.005$, $\kappa=0.9$, and no value or target clipping. The two action-value heads $Q_{\omega,1},Q_{\omega,2}$ share this architecture and the training batches; $\bar Q$ in \cref{eq:direct-expectile} is the minimum of their target copies, and the same $\min_j Q_{\omega,j}$ is the scorer of the model-free Q-select baseline (\cref{tab:scorer-selector}).

\paragraph{Optimization.} The dynamics and value losses are summed with unit weights and optimized with Adam (learning rate $3\times10^{-4}$) at batch size $1024$ for $10^6$ steps on a single H100 MIG slice (approximately $4$--$8$ hours per dataset). The checkpoint at $10^6$ steps is used throughout. The same code path serves both training and test-time imagination, so that predicted states can be consumed directly by every frozen policy. The joint optimization described here applies to the dynamics and metric-value networks. The direct scorer is loaded from the pretrained GCIQL checkpoint and remains frozen during model training.
\section{Evaluation Protocol, Statistics, and the Static Per-Episode Oracle}
\label[appendix]{app:bank-stats}

\paragraph{Evaluation protocol and statistics.}
Every number follows the official OGBench evaluation: five goals per dataset, $50$ episodes per goal, deterministic actions ($250$ episodes per bank seed, $750$ per method), with every bank policy, \wmpa{}, and \randomswitch{} on the same fixed episode seeds, so all comparisons are paired per episode. Uncertainty is a hierarchical bootstrap of per-episode differences over bank seeds then episodes ($10^4$ resamples, 95\% percentile intervals); a difference is significant when its interval excludes zero. 

\Cref{tab:bank-stats} lists, per dataset, the bank statistics of \cref{sec:when}. $B_{\max}$, $D_{\mathrm{gap}}$ are computed from the scores of \cref{tab:main}. Because every bank member is evaluated on the same \OracleEpisodes{} episodes per dataset ($5$ goals $\times$ \OracleEpsPerGoal{} episodes $\times$ $3$ bank seeds) as \wmpa{} and \randomswitch{}, the static per-episode oracle needs no additional runs: $U$ is the mean over these episodes of $\max_i \mathbf{1}[\text{policy } i \text{ succeeds}]$, and $U-B_{\max}$ is the headroom available to any per-episode selector that does not switch within an episode. When we say that \wmpa{} exceeds the oracle, we count datasets where it does so by at least one point (\NumAboveUnion{} datasets); it is numerically above $U$ on \NumAboveUnionAny{}, the remainder being floor or saturated datasets where the difference is below one point. As a pre-specified criterion, we also report the paired hierarchical bootstrap of the per-episode difference \wmpa$-U$ on the same episodes: the interval excludes zero upward on \NumAboveUnionSig{} datasets and downward on \NumBelowUnionSig{} (the latter concentrated in the dominance, floor, and saturation regimes of \cref{sec:when}).
